So a lot of pesticides are neurotoxins and will affect, even if they don't kill a bee, if it's a dose that doesn't harm them, they have these sublethal effects where they can affect their navigation, their ability to reproduce, to smell and sense other plants. So pesticides have this range of effects on honeybees and native bees. And there's this new class of insecticides that's come under a lot of scrutiny that people have probably heard of, called neonicotinoids. They're a synthetic nicotine derivative. So tobacco plants create nicotine as a chemical defense against pests. So it's a pretty potent natural pesticide that was synthesized in the early 1990s and came on the market first as imidacloprid. And the EPA just released a study that preliminarily finds that this is pretty toxic to bees. There's this amazing bee researcher in Britain named Dave Goulson who, when he gives talks, says that just a gram of imidacloprid is enough to kill 250,000 honeybees. That's a lot of bees for a very, very small amount. So they're incredibly toxic.

And initially, the attraction with neonicotinoids was that they didn't seem that they would affect things up the food chain, right? Remember DDT in Silent Spring? That was a pesticide that really amplified as it went up the food chain, affecting bird nesting, affecting mammals. And it seems like now, unfortunately, there's more evidence showing that neonicotinoids also affect things besides invertebrates. But even for invertebrates, these are water-soluble chemicals. They're pervasive in our waterways. Studies have found them in every river where they looked in the Midwest, which is pretty scary. And they're actually more toxic to aquatic invertebrates.

But because they're water-soluble, they're also a class of pesticide that's known as a systemic. This means that they're transported throughout an entire plant. So if you apply it to their leaf, they could get expressed in the pollen. And so bees will forage on a plant and be consuming small quantities of the pollen and nectar containing neonicotinoids. So even if they aren't directly sprayed, right, they could experience the sublethal effects that I described. And so that's part of why people are so concerned about them, why there's a moratorium on them in Europe, why the state of Maryland just banned them, not for farming, but for ornamental use, and why California is also considering legislation banning them right now.

**Chelsea Wills:** So do all pesticides affect bees the same ways? And once again, in your imagination, what would it look like to have a field that a conventional farmer did spray sometimes? And what would be the ideal conditions to do that? Or how does that work? Is it an absolute?

Green being the ones that you can spray and bees shouldn't really be harmed, versus red would be the ones to avoid at all costs. The yellow ones are more interesting. They might, say, be very toxic for a short period, but have a short residual toxicity, so they would dissipate from the environment quickly. So for those, you might be able to spray them at night when bees aren't active. And then, given the right conditions, they wouldn't be available the next day, and not that many bees would be harmed.

So often for producers, we ask them to think about what the risk is to bees, to look at that and then decide when to spray. Maybe don't spray during bloom; spray after bloom when bees aren't present. There are some chemicals, when you mix them, they become more toxic. So avoiding those mixtures, spraying at night, and of course, always calibrating their spray equipment and spraying during appropriate conditions where there's not a high chance of drift or inversion that could carry the pesticides miles away. So those are some of the things we recommend.

And I work for a great nonprofit right now. And we go through a pesticide report with a farmer, suggest alternatives, suggest when they should spray, and also work with them on non-chemical mechanisms. So some farmers will vacuum a crop. This happens a lot in strawberry. So it's a physical means of getting rid of a pest. There are pheromone traps that are insect-specific, so only the males will go to that trap and you can reduce their populations. But there's also conservation biocontrol. Basically, often you plant the same habitats that support pollinators, support beneficial insects, ladybugs, right? Lacewings. These are predaceous ground beetles. They'll go to these habitats and they go out and do natural pest control. Predaceous ground beetles will go out and kill slugs, and they're actually so aggressive they'll kill more prey than they can even eat, which is a farmer's dream, right? So we try and promote this suite of methods whereby farmers can reduce the need to apply pesticides. And then when they are applying it, do it in a much smarter way that doesn't harm the insects that they want to promote on their farm.

**Chelsea Wills:** And obviously that has less environmental impact, or there's less risk of other things going wrong, too, right? With less pesticide use all around. Is this the program that is the pollinator conservation program? Is that the same thing?

**Hillary Sardiñas:** At the Xerces Society? Yeah. So I work for this amazing nonprofit. We like to think of ourselves as the Audubon Society of insects. We do a lot of supporting research, a lot of habitat work, and a lot of policy. **Hillary Sardiñas:** Yeah, that's a great question. So in my home, we only had a tiny parking strip between where we park and the sidewalk.
