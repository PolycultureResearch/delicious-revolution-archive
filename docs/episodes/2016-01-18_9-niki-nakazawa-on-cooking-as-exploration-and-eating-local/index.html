Niki Nakazawa

On cooking as exploration, and eating local in Mexico City

January 18, 201640 min

Download MP3 Read the transcript

Since moving to Mexico City in 2007, Niki Nakazawa has navigated between the art, architecture, music and food worlds. After several years working as managing editor at art and architecture publishing houses, she founded the experimental pop-up restaurant and catering company Pichón with Emma Rosenbush and Kenny Curran. Pichón is a pop-up restaurant and a project dedicated to culinary research and experimentation. It is inspired by the chinampas of Mexico City, the culinary traditions of the Mexican countryside, and the gastronomic revolution that has transformed food culture. They believe that the best food is prepared with ingredients grown locally and sustainably, and that food should be a vehicle for strengthening community, from its cultivation to consumption. Chelsea talks to Nikki about Chinampas, the island-gardens of the Mexican highlands, about what it means to eat local in Mexico City, about cooking as an exploration, and about her recent culinary residency in coastal Oaxaca.

Chelsea Wills: Can you describe what they are for people who don't know what a chinampa is?

Niki Nakazawa: Sure. So this is a pre-Hispanic farming technique. I guess it dates back to almost 7,000 years ago. They were agricultural islands that were formed by sinking boats or reed structures and using the mud from the bottom of the canals to sink them, and then mixing them with a lot of organic material and hay and whatever they found. And then after they built them up enough, what they would do is plant these willow trees called ahuejotes, and these trees have a specific type of root structure which would compact the earth together. And so this is one of the earliest systems of intended irrigation.

And Nezahualcóyotl, who was the Nahuatl emperor and leader in the 1500s, he was slightly pre-conquest, he had created a system of dams in the lake system, which protected and sheltered this area from the salty waters of the northern part of Lake Texcoco. But once the Spanish came, they started taking down this system of levees and dams, and they started draining the water. And so this system was lost for a period. And then over the course of hundreds of years, there's been various regimens of water in Mexico City in which they drained the water and then in two-weighted or put the underground springs and rivers that feed this canal system, water system. And so it's a long story, but basically they're agricultural islands that date back.

And for me, whenever I've been there, I always imagine them as, they're not floating. They are actually like islands built in a lake, but they're always being constructed, right? So there's always soil being added to the top. And they're incredibly fertile, dense, little agricultural places that are managed in all of these different ways that are as unique as all the different people who live there.
