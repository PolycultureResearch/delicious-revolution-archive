On an ecological approach to environmental justice in the San Joaquin Valley

May 13, 201848 min

Download MP3 Read the transcript

Janaki Jagannath is the former Coordinator at the Community Alliance for Agroecology, a coalition of community-based organizations in the San Joaquin Valley of California that work to advance agricultural and environmental policy towards justice for communities bearing the burden of California’s food system. Prior to this, she worked at California Rural Legal Assistance in Fresno, enforcing labor standards and environmental justice protections such as access to clean drinking water for farmworker communities. Janaki has assisted in curriculum development for the Sustainable Agriculture and Food Systems degree at UC Davis and has farmed diversified and orchard crops across the state, including conducting training at the Refugee Entrepreneurial Agriculture Project in San Diego County. Janaki holds a B.S. in Agricultural Development from UC Davis and a producers’ certification in Ecological Horticulture from UC Santa Cruz Center for Agroecology. She is currently pursuing her J.D. at King Hall. In this episode, Janaki talks to Devon about organizing in the San Joaquin Valley, building movements in the legacy of the United Farm Workers, and an ecological approach to environmental justice.

Devon Sampson: Welcome to Delicious Revolution, Janaki. Thanks for being here. So, Janaki, I just wanted to start with introducing us to the place that you've been working in the San Joaquin Valley. Where is this place and what role does it play in thinking about organizing and in food systems and agriculture?

Janaki Jagannath: Well, the San Joaquin Valley is this large area of land in the middle of the state stretching from the northern Stanislaus County all the way down to Kern County, which is the county right before you get into Los Angeles. I like to think of it as this playground for what agricultural development in the United States could look like, because it's been, in some ways, products of the imagination of our universities and our policymakers and farmers, of course, and, of course, farm workers in creating the most agriculturally productive region dollar acre value wise in the country.

In terms of where it is socioeconomically and environmentally, it's often understood as being one of the most impacted places in the United States. And, of course, when you look at indicators like the Human Development Index, some of the census tracts with the worst outcomes, whether it's for education attainment or for birth weight and asthma rates, things like that, they're actually located in this region in the middle of California, which the rest of the country by and large considers to be a very affluent place.

Devon Sampson: I wanted to ask a little bit more about what you were saying about it's been the place where imaginations about agriculture are played out.

Janaki Jagannath: Yeah, what I mean by that is that there has been a very distinct imagination around agriculture in California that has been dominated by a few specific players and specifically people with the power and the economic wherewithal to assert their imagination into the landscape. And I use that analogy because it's important for us to always remember that our landscapes that we live and walk on are very much a product of certain people's idea of what they want this built environment to look like.

And when you drive through the San Joaquin Valley from north to south, you're traveling through native land. You're traveling through lands that have been worked by successions of immigrants from all over the world. And today you're driving through communities that are predominantly Latino, predominantly Spanish speaking and indigenous language speaking from the southern states of Oaxaca. And today the imagination of what agriculture looks like still looks like a dominion of a certain small set of white land holders who have asserted their power over growing a certain variety of commodity crops and actually not living on site a lot of the time, just utilizing this landscape as a capitalistic treadmill of production.

Janaki Jagannath: Well, I ended up there really by my initial work with California Rural Legal Assistance. And California Rural Legal Assistance is a legal services organization that has been around now for over 50 years. And the group has its most attended, most utilized office, the migrant unit in Fresno. And of course, these offices are located in places that are predominantly populated by farm workers.

You just need to have your head on straight and have your heart in the right place. And I really took that to heart. And I had interned previously for California Rural Legal Assistance when I was an undergraduate at UC Davis. But I decided to actually apply and move to Fresno and do some of this work more intentionally.

And when I got there, I was given a remarkable amount of responsibility at a relatively young age, I was operating a program called the Community Equity Initiative out of Fresno, which was servicing what we call disadvantaged, unincorporated communities. And these are farm worker towns that are located in predominantly agricultural areas. And the people that live there are folks that work in the fields and also are by and large people who have worked entire lives in the fields and have made enough money to purchase a small home or little trailer home.

They're dealing with really high levels of particulate matter in the air from the trucking of food products back and forth from up and down the state and out of state. Whether that's an act of overt discrimination is something that we're yet to really state, I think, legally.

But what we can understand through the land use history of California is that specific pockets of farm workers have been left out of having access to basic services and things like water, things like sidewalks and streetlights, things like basic protective housing from the elements, especially in light of climate change. Those things get actually very lethal. So Community Equity Initiative was focused on ameliorating some of those damages. And so I had the opportunity to work with a number of small towns scattered across Fresno County that were dealing with a lot of those challenges.

Janaki Jagannath: Well, the farm worker community is predominantly Mexican and Central American today. And all of those things are hugely important to look at in terms of understanding our current political moment. But there is, of course, a reality that we all have to wake up to that the communities that are most impacted in environmental justice areas of rural California are still Mexican and Central American.

Devon Sampson: And so tell me about the Community Alliance for Agroecology. I know it's a coalition of six groups.

Janaki Jagannath: Yeah. So the alliance was formed in 2015 and really came out of a much longer prior process among these six environmental justice organizations. And these organizations are Community Water Center, the Center on Race, Poverty and the Environment, Californians for Pesticide Reform and a number of other groups that have very historic linkages with farmworker communities through a variety of environmental campaigns related to pesticide exposure, to access to clean drinking water and the siting of hazardous waste facilities and oil and gas extraction.

So one might wonder how all of these different areas of environmental advocacy link together in around agriculture. But what we came together understanding was that agriculture is not an abstract concept in the San Joaquin Valley, maybe the way it is in urban places where maybe in the more urban areas of California, there's a robust dialogue about fresh and sustainable agriculture and organic and these concepts that, even as I say them, I'm realizing they might be a little bit passé now in urban communities.

However, in rural California, farming is fundamental to everything. It's the economic driver, and it's really the place where we all live. If you live in rural California, you're going to be in proximity to a farming operation. And additionally, maybe the most important point is that our elected officials and policymakers are by and large people who are constantly in flux through this revolving door between large agricultural interest and elected office. And so when it comes to actually pushing any policy change around pesticide exposures, around oil and gas extraction, around the water runoff from large dairy farms, we're always talking about power holding by those people who are landholders and who are participating in agribusiness.

So our alliance came together with that focus of not just talking about promoting organic or sustainable or any single air quotable concept of agriculture. We're more discussing creating large scale political shift through introducing alternative forms of dialogue around agriculture, but most importantly, introducing farmworker communities and small scale farmers, farmers of color, into decision making process alongside and maybe in place of some of the larger and more economically well funded decision makers that currently wield the most power.

Devon Sampson: I guess this is the big question. How do you do that? We're interested in talking about organizing and how we build political power and movements. What do you use and what inspires those approaches?

Janaki Jagannath: Yeah, well, how do we do it is very slowly, very slowly is what we've realized. And of course, the movement towards political shift is really very geologic in a sense. And I use that word because we're talking about a change that begins with the soil in the movement that we're building. As Community Alliance for Agroecology, we really deeply incorporate sustainable and agroecological methods into a larger framework of justice. Not simply, as I said before, vying for one singular form of agriculture, but rather incorporating agriculture as the most intimate relationship that human beings have with the earth into a larger framework of equity.

And none of this would be possible without the past wins of the environmental justice movement that we build off of. And the groups that formed the alliance are ones that have played a really large role in advancing environmental justice law in California, in creating some of our most important statutes that protect our groundwater, that protect our air quality, and even requiring the appointment and hire of environmental justice employees at our departments. And so it's important for us to always be remembering that we're building off of those wins to actually advance maybe more holistic framework around agricultural equality.

And in terms of getting there, some of the methods that we employ are direct organizing, of course, by actually working one-on-one with local farmworker resident communities that are dealing with the outcomes of the large industrial ag operations. And like my mentor, Lupe Martinez says, from the Center on Race, Poverty, and the Environment, he's an organizer with Cesar Chavez. He and Gustavo Aguirre, both of them are two of our mentors around organizing at the Alliance.

And they talk about this concept of education of the heart, meaning that there's a great deal of education book learning that we can do. And there's certainly a lot of book learning about climate change that needs to be done by our elected officials who actually are tasked with protecting the climate. But there's a very important other side of learning about ecology and agricultural development, which is related to our own human connection to the planet. Heart knowledge.

And what the Alliance employed was and continues to employ is conducting trainings for our community residents in the form of crash courses around agroecology. So that means identifying certain areas of the ecology, whether that's our water cycle or carbon cycle, and actually identifying where in that cycle has it been fractured by industrial agriculture, parsing it apart, looking at what are the different policy implications, what led us here, and how do we change it?

So those sorts of forums have been incredibly important for just elevating our level of literacy as advocates around simultaneously discussing science and policy and learning to remember that we too are experts, that it's not only policymakers in Sacramento or scientists in a lab that are able to come up with robust justifications for the reasons why our state looks the way it does, but that it's in fact community residents who have been working in the field who really have and should have a say as well.

Devon Sampson: Absolutely. Yeah, as one of those scientists who studies these things, I totally agree. I guess this would be a good time to ask about the Organizer Academy that I just read a little bit about. What is it and what are you guys doing?

Janaki Jagannath: Well, so the Organizer Academy concept was, like I said, born out of this hope that we build organizers within the alliance, beginning with our own staff of our six organizations, that are able to employ a uniform organizing strategy that then will translate into policy change and ultimately shifting decision-making in terms of who is in elected office.

And it's a very long-term goal that I'm hoping, as the state begins to awaken to the disparities that the San Joaquin Valley faces, that there's also a solidarity between urban and rural communities that begins to build through our rural organizers taking a message out to the broader state and also being able to have this exchange between the urban and rural communities in terms of some of these agroecology encuentros or meetups that we've been hosting.

So right now, the Organizer Academy really is localized in the San Joaquin Valley, but I think the hope is that long-term, it will expand the reach of some of our environmental concerns to really meet some of the movement that's taking place or maybe the dialogue that's happening around sustainable agriculture and healthy food in urban areas.

Devon Sampson: How do you see that happening? It strikes me as one of the most important things that movement building is, is figuring out those ways to work and find alliances across difference like that. How do you see building that kind of alliance from rural and urban places? Or how do you see the voices of people in rural places reaching a larger audience?

Janaki Jagannath: Well, I think the most important way is for really deliberate spaces to be made at the decision-making table as well as in publication and in universities to actually hear from impacted people from the valley for the first-hand experience to be articulated. And I always say it's 2018 and it's time for urban people to be teaching other urban people about what's going on in California. And it's a little bit tired relying on rural people to continually be the ones who are driving hours and hours to universities and to different conferences to say we're here and this is what's happening in rural California. It's about time that space actually gets made for those people to be in positions of decision-making and to have the education be going on among urban people.

I think although we really, of course, are comfortably a blue state, we do take a lot of heart in this idea that it's an edifice of liberal thinking and that it's monolithic rather than really looking at, actually much more troubling is looking at some of the smaller communities that are primarily agricultural, the people that have been most welcoming of President Trump when he comes to California, and some of the institutions that are the foundational icons of rural California.

So all of that is to say that as urban California starts to reckon with this, I think it's really important that rather than simply continuing to drive on the I-5 with your eyes partially closed between Los Angeles and the Bay Area, actually get off on some of these side streets, eat some bomb tacos on the side of the road, talk to people, and begin to develop a much more authentic connection, if not friendship with actual people, at least an awareness of what the landscape that's largely a sacrificial landscape has, what role that's played in providing food, fiber, and fuel for not only the state, but for the entire country.

Devon Sampson: Yeah, sure. I wanted to ask a little bit about the Farmer Equity Act, too, as an example of policy change. What is it? And it was recently signed into law, am I right?

Janaki Jagannath: Yeah, it was signed into law at the end of last year, and it's the Farmer Equity Act of 2017. And the law is the first of its kind in the United States, in the sense that it's the first state law to codify socially disadvantaged farmers and ranchers under our state California Food and Agriculture Code. So what that does is it adds a definition of a socially disadvantaged farmer, and of course, that includes a short list of ethnicities.

And with this passage of this bill, the idea is that it will open the door to further potential set-asides when the time comes that California has a greater number of state grant programs for agriculture, and especially for conservation, which communities of color have been doing for many, many, many, many years, when it comes to protecting our soils, those kinds of programs that are there subsidizing practices that are sometimes indigenous practices, that are sometimes practices that farmers of color are already utilizing or have brought with them from their country of origin.

This act is positioning them such that they can receive those benefits and also so that they don't fall through the cracks in terms of in this way that, hearkening back to this imagination of California agriculture, there tends to always be this treadmill of development where a certain form of agriculture is piloted, and then it's adopted, there's an adoption phase, and there's research and development, and then there's technological development, and this treadmill continues, it's just barreling down the racetrack of building the next big tractor, creating the next most effective pesticide, the next most efficient means of agricultural production. And of course, the people who always fall through the cracks of any of those subsidies or in that development treadmill are always the smallest producers who are by and large farmers of color.

And so what this bill is intended to do is that in this moment of California creating programs such as the Healthy Soils Initiative, which is meant to protect California soils and to improve our soil organic matter, that when such conservation programs are released, that there be at least a definition for what a socially disadvantaged farmer is, so that looking into the future, we can consider how the state might actually set aside funds specifically for those practitioners who have been historically left out.

Devon Sampson: And that is the history of a lot of these things are leaving out people of color from agricultural support and all the subsidies that go into it.

Janaki Jagannath: Yeah, of course, those outcomes can be tied back to property ownership in California and really from the very beginning. Starting with the Homestead Act, which essentially subsidized white farmers to manifest destiny, to come out to the West and settle their piece of land and improve it through agriculture. And then by doing so, be given that land for free by the federal government, followed by the creation of agricultural schools, like the one I'm at here at UC Davis, it's a land grant school that's federally funded to do agricultural research.

All of these different federal programs here in the United States have been highly tailored towards the advancement of large and efficient agriculture and, like any movement that is seeking to carve out spaces for people of color and establish greater access to decision making for us, it starts out small and the Farmer Equity Act is a small change to the California Food and Agriculture Code, but it does have a couple of very important additional pieces to it that I'd like to mention, which are one that it calls for the hire of an executive position at the department to oversee outreach efforts by the department. So that means that there's going to be basically a civil rights executive position at the Department of Food and Agriculture for the first time in California.

Additionally, it calls for the department to create a report about the department's compliance with federal law related to socially disadvantaged farmers and ranchers. And it's important to mention that we have a national designation for socially disadvantaged farmer and rancher, which came out of litigation on behalf of black farmers in the South who brought suit for racial discrimination against the USDA. So we've really inherited in California through the Farmer Equity Act process, we have inherited a lot of that work that was done by those people and through that litigation process. And proud to say that California is going to be now implementing some of those values on a state level.
